Software and the City*

May 29, 2026

Personal software is the new self-expression.

In Season 2, Episode 1 of Sex and the City, Carrie says in her opening voiceover: “In New York, they say you’re always looking for a job, a boyfriend, or an apartment.” But let’s face it, the real search is for a way to express yourself.

In my mid-twenties in San Francisco, Carrie’s sentiment echoes. However, I felt the question answered when we could build software for ourselves. Instead of journaling every night before bed, I began to build products for ideas that had been on my backburner. Building, not writing, became my newest obsession.

My profound nighttime routine shift inspired me to attend community events to seek out others who felt this too. As I boarded the Embarcadero-bound MUNI to attend Claude Code For Normies in March hosted by Nikunj, Sara, and Michael, which brings together non-technical users to present their personal projects, I began thinking about a popular coding agent, OpenClaw. How had it managed to capture the hearts of the normies, nerds, and engineers alike?

I realized it had brought the magic of coding agents to people who’d never thought of themselves as builders, giving them a new way to express their ideas.

A few weeks later, I found myself at another CC community event — this one specifically for Women in AI. I capped the Sharpie, stuck on my name tag, and walked in. As the presentations began, I learned how Joyce had built a literacy app for American-Born Chinese wanting to improve their Mandarin reading, Julia an AI that flirts with you, Saloni a real-time stablecoin trading database across countries, and Neha a solar system explorer for kids.

It gave me a glimpse into how building had been democratized. Previously, enterprises built products for people. Now, people build products for joy.

I was inspired by the personalization, creativity, and sense of meaning everyone brought to their projects. It reminded me of Cursor’s philosophy of making coding feel more like sculpting and painting, now that the barrier to building has been lowered.

After presenting my own project, Pinch, an AI astrologer you can text — it was satisfying to be greeted by eager beta users who happened to be astrology believers, and who felt connected to its mission. I’d started building it for a better horoscope. I kept building it because designing Pinch’s voice and personality felt like a new way to express myself.

The real search continues. Carrie found hers in a newspaper column. I found mine in a terminal.
